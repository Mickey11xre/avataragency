The research behind the instruments
Every Thriving Quotient instrument rests on published, peer-reviewed research into what it means for a student to thrive in college — and on the doctoral {dissertation} studies that tested that model with specific populations. This is the record: the publications, the conference papers, the dissertations, the recordings{, }and the articles, kept here so that an institutional research office can check the instrument against the literature before {purchase}.
The research on faculty and staff thriving is much more recent, but there are numerous dissertation studies establishing the reliability and validity of each instrument.

What thriving means
[DEL:Thriving describes a student who is doing well as a whole person: engaged academically, connected interpersonally, and psychologically well. {Replace with:}
Thriving is an expanded view of student success that encompasses the whole person. Thriving students are those who are vitally engaged academically and psychologically, as well as connected interpersonally.  Thriving implies maximizing the college experience in anticipation of full engagement in a meaningful and productive life. Rather than simply surviving college, thriving is about making the most of it and fulfilling one’s potential.
In our research, there are five valid and reliable scales that measure thriving: Engaged Learning, Academic Determination, Positive Perspective, Diverse Citizenship and Social Connectedness—each of which can be affected by the institutional environment and by carefully constructed interventions. As a result, an institution can take action to affect student thriving well before the student census data conveys that a student has made the decision to leave the institution.
The word matters because it changes what a campus measures. Retention and graduation rates record what has already happened. Thriving records the condition of the student now, with sufficient detail to know what steps to take to change the outcome.

Thriving Quotient vs. NSSE
{The National Survey of Student Engagement }measures engagement: how much time and effort students put into educationally purposeful activities, and how the institution organizes {those activities}. {The survey assesses “how undergraduates spend their time and what they gain from attending college” (NSSE website). {The Thriving Quotient (TQ) measures thriving: the degree to which students are vitally engaged and optimally functioning not just behaviorally, but academically, psychologically, and interpersonally. These are psychosocial factors that are amenable to change. The TQ also measures the pathways to thriving: the statistically significant predictors of the variation in students’ thriving levels that represent areas an institution can target for intervention. These pathways include a wide variety of campus experiences, including levels of campus involvement, perceptions of faculty and classroom experiences, perceptions of institutional integrity, inclusion of diverse perspectives, and satisfaction levels with various campus programs and services. Thriving and retention or persistence
Retention and persistence are {common ways of measuring student success}[DEL:. outcomes:] [DEL:the student re-enrolled, or did not. They are counted after the fact and cannot be acted on for the student who has already left.] Retention is what the institution does (the institution retains a student from the first to the second year); persistence is what the student does (persists from year to year until graduation). Each of these outcomes is measured after the fact: retention is measured one year after initial enrollment, and persistence to graduation is measured after a student graduates (or doesn’t). In contrast, thriving is measured while the student is enrolled, so it functions as a leading indicator: it points to the students and the experiences where action is still possible.
Thriving and belonging
Sense of Belonging in college is usually defined as students’ sense of mattering, feeling connected to, and being supported by faculty, staff, and peers within an institution. It is connected to thriving in two ways: first, the Social Connectedness scale of the Thriving Quotient measures students’ sense of feeling supported and connected in healthy ways to their peers; second, one of the major pathways to thriving measured on the instrument is a Psychological Sense of Community, defined as feeling one belongs on campus, is proud to attend the institution, and that being a student there is meeting important needs in their life. Thriving adds the academic and psychological elements to the interpersonal, and expands beyond belonging to include students’ sense of ownership.
[DEL:is close to one factor of thriving, Social Connectedness: the presence of meaningful relationships and a felt sense of belonging on campus. Thriving adds the academic dimension (Engaged Learning, Academic Determination) and the psychological dimension (Positive Perspective, Diverse Citizenship) alongside it, so a campus can see whether a belonging problem is the whole picture or one part of it.]
Thriving and student success
Student success is usually defined by grades, retention {rates, and persistence to graduation}. Thriving {expands that }definition: it {encompasses} those outcomes and the psychological engagement, connection{,} and well{-}being that precede them.

{Pathways to thriving}
{Examining thriving among community college students developed out of a desire to understand student success more holistically than previous higher education research that had focused solely on student retention. Dr. Amy Azul revised the Thriving Quotient developed by Dr. Laurie Schreiner to fit the community college student’s experience. The items that measure thriving are the same, but the pathways predictive of thriving in the community college setting are different. The Thriving Quotient™ (TQ) uses concepts from positive psychology (i.e., flourishing) and Bean and Eaton’s (2000) retention model to assess thriving, defined as “the experiences of college students who are fully engaged intellectually, socially, and emotionally” (Schreiner, 2010, p. 4). Thriving is not about personality traits, but rather describes malleable qualities within a student, meaning that institutions can teach students to thrive. Dr. Azul’s research has documented specific experiences that serve as significant pathways to student thriving in the community college environment; these pathways are also measured in the online survey.}
