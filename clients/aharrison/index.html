What changed in Version 2 — and why: this is the style you chose on September 4th, drawn for the first time. The pencil-drawn “duck book” look from Adele’s references, in the Jan Brett family — deliberately not another AI picture book, but something meant to read as a classic in twenty years.

The rule underneath it: only the characters are in colour. Everything around them — the rooms, the props, the people — stays in warm sepia and greyscale. That came from Bill’s observation that dogs don’t see in colour, which turns a style decision into part of the story: the monochrome world is how Lady Belle sees it. It is also why humans stay knees-down and hands only, so that, in Adele’s words, every child can put themselves in that family.

One consequence worth knowing: a white dog in a sepia world has no colour of her own, so Belle’s red collar and gold “Kindness” tag are what mark her as the colour character. Bill’s collar idea turned out to be structural, not decorative. Belle now sits on her bed in the family room with the happier, open-mouth expression from the original concept; the cats are shown as solo seated portraits, both squared to camera, so each gets as much detail as she does. Shadow’s eyes are yellow and Cuddles’ are green, per your canon, and Cuddles has his stubby bobbed tail.

The concept: Lady Belle at the centre, ringed by the nine fruits of the Spirit, with Fruit of the Spirit as the series line beneath the title. She wears the same red collar and “Kindness” tag as the character art, so the book’s first virtue is carried in the mark itself.

Two versions, one decision. Belle and the fruit are identical in both — the same drawing, in the same places. The only thing that changes is what holds the ring together. Version 1 uses the pale blue ribbon weaving between the fruits. Version 2 takes the ribbon away and replaces it with warm light coming from behind her, so the fruits sit in the glow rather than on a ribbon. Belle herself stays white in both.

Version 3 keeps the light from version 2 and puts the fruits the way you asked on our October 15 call: evenly spaced like the numbers on a clock, in the order the books will be published, with the apple for Kindness at twelve o’clock.

What to look at: the fruits are the ring, so their choice and order are worth a careful look — whether these are the nine you want, and whether any of them should change. Then the type: the title face, the weight of Fruit of the Spirit beneath it, and whether Belle reads clearly when this is printed small on a spine or a website header.

One thing we deliberately watched: Bill raised the risk that a circular ribbon could read as another well-known logo. In version 1 the ribbon is drawn soft and broken, weaving behind separate pieces of fruit rather than as a continuous swirling emblem. Version 2 has no ribbon at all. If either still feels close to you, say so now — it is far easier to change here than after it is on a cover.

What to listen for. All three read the same words, and we have set them to the same volume on purpose — so that the one you prefer is the one you genuinely prefer, and not simply the one that happened to be recorded loudest.

What you are looking at. Pages 4 and 5 of Adele’s manuscript (the version dated August 26). On page 4, Lady Belle leaves her kennel and begs the caretaker to turn toward the solid door. On page 5 the caretaker does, and she bounces down the hall toward it — so we drew page 5 from behind them, walking toward the big, solid door, the way the words tell it. We see her from the rear, prancing, because the moment is about where she is going and who might be waiting behind that door.

Why these two pages. In the printed book they face each other (book pages 8 and 9), so this is a true spread, not two pictures placed side by side. They are still two separate illustrations, one per page, as we agreed — but drawn as one continuous hallway, with the same kennels, floor and morning light, so the eye travels from her kennel on the left to the door on the right, in the direction of the story and of the page turn. They are also the first pages where a person appears, which made them the right test of how we handle the humans.
The style. It follows the character art you approved: brown pencil and crayon hatching on warm tan paper, with only Lady Belle in color. The caretaker, the leash, the other dogs and the shelter are all in sepia — the world as a dog sees it. People appear only as legs and hands, never faces, so every child can picture their own family in the story. Where the words sit, the drawing goes lighter, with fewer pencil strokes, so the text reads easily without a box or a panel over the picture.

The type. Adele’s words are set in Assistant, the clean, friendly typeface Brave Books uses — the look you pointed us to — at 16 point on 22 point line spacing and left-aligned: a comfortable size for a parent reading aloud and a child following along. The words sit in a different place on each page and balance each other across the spread — at the top of page 4 beside the caretaker, and in a band along the bottom of page 5, beneath the scene.

Two small text notes for Adele. We set your words exactly as written, with one fix: page 4 was missing a closing quotation mark after “solid door!”, so we added it. Two spellings are yours to decide, and we have not changed them: “zig zagging” is usually written as one word, “zigzagging,” and “lady-like” as “ladylike.”
Book size · Amazon KDP print specifications
We are designing Book One as an 8.5 × 8.5 inch square book, printed in full color through Amazon KDP. Every page, including the spread above, is drawn to these measurements.
Trim size (the finished page)8.5 × 8.5 in
Page with bleed8.625 × 8.75 in — the art runs 0.125 in past the outside, top and bottom edges, so no white edge shows after trimming
Resolution300 DPI: 2,588 × 2,625 px per page; a two-page spread is 5,175 × 2,625 px
Page count32 pages, the standard for picture books (KDP’s paperback minimum is 24, and books bind in multiples of 4). Adele’s 23 story pages, plus the title, copyright, dedication, verse and note pages, fill it.
InteriorPremium color
Safe marginsWords, faces and key details stay at least 0.375 in from the trim and from the fold
Why 8.5 × 8.5. It is the classic square picture-book size, the shape of many of the read-aloud books on a family’s shelf: large enough for the illustrations to carry the story, small enough for a child’s lap. A square page works equally well for a single illustration and for two facing pages read together, as in this spread. KDP prints it as a full-color paperback.

If you also want a hardcover. KDP’s hardcovers start at 75 pages and don’t come in square sizes, so a 32-page hardcover would need a second printer, such as IngramSpark. We would confirm their square-format options first; the aim is the same 8.5 × 8.5 size and the same artwork for both editions.

Why we decide it now. The shape of the page sets the composition of every illustration. The other common choice is a portrait 8 × 10 in book, which leaves more room for text under the pictures; we recommend the square. Once the remaining pages are drawn, changing the size would mean recomposing all of them, so this is the moment to confirm it.

What we need from you: your approval of (1) the illustration style on real pages, (2) the type and where the words sit, and (3) the 8.5 × 8.5 size — or your notes on any of them. Notes now cost far less than notes after all the pages are drawn.
