Your new website — the plan, and what we need from you

How thrivingincollege.org is being rebuilt for September 2026: what the site will contain, how people will find it and buy from it, what happens after launch — and the handful of decisions only you can make.

Prepared by: Michael Rivera & Stephanie Geter September 2026 Target launch: September 15, 2026

Dr. Schreiner — before designing anything, we read every page currently published on your site, including several that are live but no longer reachable from your menu. That reading is what this document is built on.

Fifteen years of work has accumulated on that site, and in a few places it now says two different things — a price that appears one way on one page and another way in the store, a reliability figure that differs between two pages describing the same instrument. None of this is unusual, and none of it is a criticism. It simply means that in a handful of places, only you know which version is the right one.

So this document does two things. The first half describes what we are building and why. The second half — What we need from you — is a short set of questions and materials. Short answers are perfectly fine: a number, a "yes," or "use the second one" is usually all we need. We will walk through them with you together; nothing here needs answering in advance.

The one-sentence version. Your research, your instruments and your reputation are the strongest assets in this field, and the current site makes people work to reach them. The new site puts your evidence in front of the decision, your prices in one place, and a working store where a campus can act the moment it decides to.

Section 01

What we're building

The design you approved — the seed that becomes a rooted tree as you scroll, the greens, the butterfly, the film above the fold — is the right homepage. What it needs beneath it is a site: pages for every instrument, one clear place to see prices, a store that works, and the consulting and speaking work that today has no page at all.

Five decisions shape everything that follows.

  1. Navigation organised around your products, with audiences as a second layer. Instruments · Packages & Pricing · Thriving Knowledge Center · Consulting & Speaking · About, plus a visible Store. This is how the instrument providers your buyers already know are organised. "For campus leaders / For researchers / For faculty & staff" appears as a set of cards on the homepage, which is where audience routing works well, rather than as the menu itself, where it reliably confuses people.
  2. One pricing page and one store. Every package on one comparison table, and a single storefront reached from the main menu — with a "Buy" button in the same position on every product page that jumps straight to that product. One place to look, one place to update.
  3. Two ways to pay, on every package. A card checkout for the campuses that buy on a purchasing card, and a quote-and-invoice path with a PO number, Net 30 terms and bank transfer for the ones that must go through procurement. Your peers offer one or the other; offering both is a genuine advantage.
  4. Delivery happens by itself. Where an item can be sent instantly, the buyer receives it the moment payment clears — and you are not in that loop. Where an item is custom, the store collects what you need to begin the work.
  5. Your evidence comes before the ask. On the homepage, on the product pages, and in the way the whole site is written, the research arrives before the price. For a purchase of this size, that ordering is the difference between interest and confidence.

One note on approach: the instrument providers closest to your work — NSSE at Indiana, CCSSE at UT Austin, Gallup's education programme — all publish their prices openly and let institutions register themselves. The consultancies hide pricing behind a sales conversation. You have fixed, published prices from $250 to $10,000, which puts you firmly in the first group, and the new site is built to make that transparency an advantage rather than an accident.

Section 04

How people will buy — and what happens next

Two things change here. Campuses will be able to complete a purchase whichever way their institution pays; and once they have paid, the site — not you — takes care of what happens next wherever that is possible.

Two ways to pay

University purchasing cards typically cap at $2,500 to $5,000 per transaction, which covers every package except the Thriving Campus. So the site offers both paths on every package, and the buyer chooses.

By card
For a department buying on a purchasing card: a short checkout, the package plus any instrument add-ons, and a receipt. Minutes, not weeks.
By quote and invoice
For procurement: a formal quote with a reference number for their budget packet, converted to an invoice with their PO number, Net 30, payable by card or bank transfer.
Both arrive in the same place
Whichever path a campus takes, delivery and set-up work identically afterwards. One pipeline, not two.

What happens after payment

This is the part of the current site that is missing, and it is where the rebuild gives you back the most time. Your products fall into three groups, and the store will know which group each item belongs to.

TypeExamplesWhat the buyer receives, and when
Ready nowDocuments and anything that already exists as a fileA download link on the confirmation screen the moment payment clears, and the same link in the receipt email so it survives a closed browser tab. No step of yours involved.
ScheduledSurvey administrations and report packagesThe confirmation screen says plainly what happens next and by when, then a short form collects the administration details — so you receive one complete brief rather than a thread of emails.
Built for themThe predictive model, and anything drawn from a campus's own dataThese can't be handed over at purchase, because the work begins once the survey data exists. Payment is taken, then the buyer answers the questions you currently ask by email, and the confirmation sets expectations about timing.
What will be true on September 15, and what follows shortly after. At launch, the store is live, fully branded to your new site, and taking orders: one storefront, a buy button on every product, both payment paths, and automatic delivery for everything that can be delivered automatically. The more sophisticated part — where the questions a buyer is asked change depending on which package they bought, and the whole custom-work intake runs itself — lands shortly after launch.

That order is deliberate. This is your buying season, and a working, well-presented store in the middle of it is worth more than a perfect one in October. We would rather tell you that now than have it be a surprise later.

One practical matter: the Stripe account

The new store runs on Stripe, and the account has to be opened by you, in your name — Stripe verifies the legal entity that receives the money, which is not something that can be delegated, and shouldn't be. An account opened under our name would route your revenue through our business, which neither of us wants. It takes about fifteen minutes and needs your business details and bank information.

Once it exists, you add us as a team member from your dashboard so we can build the store inside it. Worth saying plainly: you never share a password, the invitation is individually revocable at any moment, every action any team member takes is recorded in your security history, and ownership, payouts and the ability to close the account remain yours alone.

We will also need to agree what happens to PayPal during the change-over — whether it stays live while the new store beds in, and who watches anything in flight.

Section 05

The Thriving Knowledge Center

Your publications, presentations and doctoral studies are, collectively, something almost no instrument in this field can match. At the moment they exist as three lists ordered by date — which answers only one question, "what is newest," and answers it least well of all.

The Knowledge Center reorganises the same material around the question people actually arrive with: what does the research say about students like mine?

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Learner first, year secondThe top level is the population — sophomores, adult learners, graduate students, community college, faculty and staff, whole institutions. Inside a category you can filter by year, so a researcher looking for pandemic-era work, or for the earliest studies, still arrives in two clicks. Whether an item is a publication, a presentation or a dissertation becomes a filter rather than its address.
One home for the depthReliability figures, factor analyses, sample sizes and methodology live here in full. The instrument pages carry one clear line of evidence and a link — so someone deciding whether to survey their campus isn't reading psychometrics, and someone who wants the psychometrics finds all of it in one place.
Everything findable, obtained properlyEach item says where it is obtained. Where the work is yours to share, we host it. Where it belongs to its author or a publisher — several of the dissertations, and articles published in journals — we link to the place it is properly bought or downloaded. Items with no available file are still listed in full, with their DOI where one exists, so a reader can always find them even when they can't download them here.
An invitation, not a filing cabinetThe point of the section is that a visitor can see the shape of fifteen years of work — how much, on whom, showing what — and follow their own interest into it.
The version we most want to build comes next. The strongest possible answer here is not a better list but a picture: a way of seeing what this body of research has actually shown — the scale of it, the populations, the findings — that a provost can absorb in thirty seconds and a researcher can dig into for an hour. That is genuinely worth doing well rather than quickly, so at launch the Knowledge Center is the clean, categorised library described above, and the visual version follows in the next phase. We are collecting examples now.
Section 06

Being found — by search engines, and by AI assistants

Something has changed in how institutional buyers begin their search, and it affects you more than most. Increasingly, the first question isn't typed into Google — it's asked of an AI assistant. "How should we measure student thriving?" "What's an alternative to NSSE?" The answer that assistant gives shapes the shortlist before anyone visits a website at all.

This matters to you specifically because you created the vocabulary of this field. "Thriving" as a measurable construct, the Thriving Quotient itself — these are yours, with published, citable research behind them. The goal is straightforward: when someone asks an assistant how to measure student thriving, the answer should be your work, and it should point to your site.

Three things make that happen, and all three are being built in from the start rather than added later.

What we're doingWhy
Every word on the site exists as real text.The AI systems that read the web do not watch animations or run the code that draws them. A beautiful page whose words are painted into a picture is, to them, a blank page. So the animation sits on top of a complete, readable document rather than replacing it — which also happens to be what makes the site work for screen readers and for anyone who prefers reduced motion.
Pages that answer questions directly.A handful of new pages answer, in plain language and under their own headings, the questions your buyers actually ask: what thriving is, how it differs from retention and engagement and belonging, why sophomores slump, how the Thriving Quotient compares with NSSE. These are the pages an assistant can quote — and quoting is how you get recommended.
Your own data, presented so it can be quoted.Original figures — sample sizes, national norms, reliability — are among the strongest reasons a source gets cited rather than summarised. Yours are real and yours alone. They simply need to be published as clear, attributed statements: this figure, from this population, in this year.
One recommendation and one honest caveat. The recommendation: allow the AI companies' systems to read your site. Some site owners block them by default; in your position that would be working against your own goal, since being read is the precondition for being cited.

The caveat: a great website is the necessary half of this, not the whole of it. Some of what determines whether assistants name you happens elsewhere — in the repositories, directories and comparison articles they already rely on, several of which currently point at other institutions' copies of your instruments rather than at you. That is separate work, and we'd rather say so plainly now than let a beautiful site carry an expectation it can't meet alone.
